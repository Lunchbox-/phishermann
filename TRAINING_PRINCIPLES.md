# Security Awareness Training Principles

When designing internal security simulations, the goal is to educate, not to trick users with impossible-to-detect attacks. Focus your training on these core principles:

## 1. Verify the Source
Teach users to scrutinize the sender's email address.
*   **Display Name Spoofing:** Attackers often change the "Display Name" to match an executive (e.g., "CEO John Doe") while the actual email address is `johndoe123@gmail.com`.
*   **Lookalike Domains:** Warn users about domains that are slightly misspelled (e.g., `@pfcvaIves.com` using a capital 'I' instead of an 'l').

## 2. Beware of Emotional Triggers
Phishing relies on psychology. Train users to pause when an email evokes:
*   **Urgency/Fear:** "Your password expires in 2 hours."
*   **Greed:** "Click here to claim your Q3 bonus."
*   **Curiosity:** "Attached is the updated restructuring plan."

## 3. Inspect Links Safely
*   **Hovering:** Teach users to hover their mouse cursor over a link without clicking to reveal the actual destination URL.
*   **Context:** If an email from "Microsoft" links to a random `.ru` or `.xyz` domain, it is malicious.

## 4. Establish Out-of-Band Verification
Create a culture where it is acceptable and encouraged to verify unusual requests.
*   If the CEO emails asking for urgent wire transfers or gift cards, employees should be trained to call the CEO or message them on an internal chat platform (like Teams or Slack) to verify, rather than replying to the email.

## 5. Report, Don't Just Delete
Ensure users know *how* to report suspicious emails (e.g., a "Report Phishing" button in Outlook, or forwarding to `security@pfcvalves.com`). Deleting the email protects one user; reporting it allows IT to remove it from everyone's inbox.
